### **[AI]:
 What vLLM Actually Does**

If you are used to writing standard PyTorch inference loops, you know that processing images one by one leaves a lot of your GPU's computing power sitting idle while it waits for memory transfers.

**vLLM** is an open-source inference engine designed to completely eliminate that bottleneck. If you install it and route your model through it, it does three things under the hood:

> 1. **PagedAttention:** Standard Hugging Face models pre-allocate huge, contiguous chunks of VRAM for the KV cache (the memory the model uses to remember the context of the page). This leads to fragmentation and Out-Of-Memory (OOM) errors. vLLM breaks this memory into "pages" (like an operating system), virtually eliminating VRAM waste. This is huge for your 12GB VRAM ceiling.  
> 2. **Continuous Batching:** Instead of waiting for the longest page in a batch to finish generating before starting the next batch, vLLM dynamically pushes new pages into the GPU the exact millisecond a previous page finishes.  
> 3. **High Throughput:** By combining the two above, vLLM allows you to feed your 1337 images into the engine in massive batches, maximizing your RTX 3060's tensor cores. It can speed up extraction times by 2x to 5x compared to a standard for loop.

To put it simply:

### 1. Why is the error rate (CER) ~11.67% if the text is accurate?
The model is **not** making mistakes with the actual words. The 11.67% difference comes almost entirely from **tiny punctuation and spacing details**:

* **Dash Symbols:** The original book prints a long em-dash (`—`), but the model extracted a short hyphen (`-`).
* **Spaces around Punctuation:** The original book prints `don 't` or `Exit .`, whereas standard text uses `don't` or `Exit.`.
* **Line-ending Hyphens:** When a word is split at the end of a line (e.g. `dis-` / `position`), the model sometimes joins them or keeps the hyphen.

---

### 2. What does "2-column layout reconstructed cleanly" mean?
The Shakespeare book pages are printed in **2 parallel columns** (Left Column and Right Column).

* **Bad OCR (like Internet Archive raw OCR):** Reads left-to-right across the whole page, mixing Line 1 of Column 1 with Line 1 of Column 2 into a garbled mess.
* **Qwen3-VL Model:** Correctly reads **all of Column 1 from top to bottom first**, and then reads **all of Column 2 from top to bottom**. 

So the text order and dialogue flow are **100% intact and readable**, even if tiny punctuation symbols differ slightly!

Here is the detailed explanation of the logic behind **Semantic Chunking** and why the main book pages (`pages/` containing plays and poems) use **Structural/Rule-Based Chunking** instead.

---

### 1. The Logic Behind Semantic Chunking

Traditional chunkers use fixed-character or fixed-token windows (e.g., every 500 characters or 200 tokens). This often cuts mid-sentence or groups two completely unrelated paragraphs together.

**Semantic chunking** operates on meaning rather than character counts:

```
[Sentence 1] ───sim─── [Sentence 2] ───sim─── [Sentence 3] ───DROP─── [Sentence 4] ───sim─── [Sentence 5]
└─────────────────────── Chunk 1 ────────────────────────┘   │  └────────────────── Chunk 2 ──────────────────┘
                                                          CUT HERE
```

#### Step-by-Step Mechanism:

1. **Sentence Tokenization:** The continuous prose is first divided into standalone sentences ($S_1, S_2, \dots, S_n$).
2. **Vector Representation:** Each sentence is projected into a semantic vector space (via TF-IDF n-grams or dense embedding models like `SentenceTransformers`).
3. **Adjacent Cosine Similarity:** We compute the semantic similarity between every consecutive pair of sentences:
   $$
   \text{Similarity}(S_i, S_{i+1}) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_{i+1}}{\|\mathbf{v}_i\| \|\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\|}
   $$
4. **Boundary Detection (Similarity Drops):** High similarity means consecutive sentences are discussing the same concept. A sharp drop in similarity indicates a **topic shift** or narrative transition.
5. **Adaptive Thresholding:** We calculate a percentile threshold (e.g., bottom 10% of similarity scores for that document) to place cut points only where meaning changes significantly.
6. **Merging & Constraints:** Sentences between cut points are grouped into chunks while enforcing a minimum word floor ($\ge 40$ words) and an optimal retrieval size ($\sim 200$ words).

#### Why `bio/` (Biography & Essays) Uses Semantic Chunking:

The 37 biography pages are **unstructured continuous prose** (biographical essays by 19th-century scholars). They contain no speaker names, no act/scene demarcations, and no verse stanzas. Semantic chunking allows the model to find natural biographical topic transitions (e.g., moving from Shakespeare's childhood $\rightarrow$ his marriage $\rightarrow$ his London theatre years).

---

### 2. Why `pages/` (Plays and Poems) Are NOT Chunked Semantically

Using semantic chunking on plays and poems would degrade retrieval performance for 5 main reasons:

#### 1. The Text Already Has Explicit Printed Structure

Plays and poems already possess strong structural boundaries designed by the author and printer:

- **Plays:** `Act`, `Scene`, `Speaker Turn` (e.g., `Ham.`, `Pol.`, `King.`).
- **Sonnets:** 14-line standalone poems marked with Roman numerals (`I.`, `II.`, `XVIII.`).
- **Poems:** Stanzas separated by blank lines.

Running a semantic model to guess where one scene or speaker starts is unnecessary when the page literally prints `SCENE II.—The Castle` and `Pol.:`.

---

#### 2. Dialogue Breaks Semantic Similarity Models

In dramatic dialogue, rapid banter between characters often has radically different vocabulary from line to line:

> **Hamlet:** *Do you see yonder cloud that’s almost in shape of a camel?*
> **Polonius:** *By the mass, and ’tis like a camel, indeed.*
> **Hamlet:** *Methinks it is like a weasel.*
> **Polonius:** *It is backed like a weasel.*
> **Hamlet:** *Or like a whale?*
> **Polonius:** *Very like a whale.*

An embedding model would see questions, animals, and rhetorical pivots as erratic topic jumps and shatter this single witty exchange across multiple fragmented chunks. **Rule-based dialogue packing** keeps the entire conversational exchange together up to $\sim 200$ words.

---

#### 3. Exact Citation & Groundedness

The core promise of a Shakespeare RAG system is answering questions with exact citations:

> *"Where did Hamlet say 'To be or not to be'?"* $\rightarrow$ `[Hamlet | Act III Sc I | pp. 1145 | Ham]`

- **Rule-based chunking** never cuts mid-speech and flushes on Act/Scene boundaries. Every chunk has deterministic, verified metadata (`act`, `scene`, `speakers`, `page_start`, `page_end`).
- **Semantic chunking** can cut mid-speech or cross a scene boundary, producing corrupted citations where Hamlet's speech is partially attributed to Ophelia or merges across different acts.

---

#### 4. Sonnet and Stanza Integrity

- A Shakespearean sonnet is an indivisible 14-line poetic unit.
- Semantic chunking might split a sonnet between the octave (problem) and the sestet (resolution) because the tone shifts.
- Rule-based routing enforces **1 sonnet = 1 chunk**, preserving the entire poem for retrieval.

---

#### 5. Computational Cost & Determinism

- **Rule-based chunking:** Parses all 1,300+ play and poem pages in **$< 2$ seconds**, deterministic, $100\%$ reproducible, and requires zero model inference.
- **Semantic chunking:** Embedding tens of thousands of archaic Early Modern English verse lines across 1,300+ pages adds heavy computational overhead without improving boundary accuracy over explicit structural cues.

| ---------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| **Text Genre**         | Dramatic verse & formal poetry                  | Continuous 19th-century academic prose      |
| **Inherent Structure** | Acts, Scenes, Speaker Turns, Stanzas            | Linear narrative paragraphs                 |
| **Chunking Method**    | **Rule-Based / Structural**               | **Semantic Similarity Drops**         |
| **Boundary Rule**      | Cut on Act/Scene change & speaker turns         | Cut on steepest 10% cosine similarity drops |
| **Primary Goal**       | Exact citation metadata & dialogue preservation | Thematic coherence across continuous prose  |

### ১. বাক্যগুলোকে ভেক্টরে রূপান্তর (Vectorization)
জীবনী বা প্রবন্ধের টেক্সটকে প্রথমে প্রতিটি একক বাক্যে ($S_1, S_2, S_3, \dots, S_n$) ভাগ করা হয়। এরপর প্রতিটি বাক্যকে ভেক্টর স্পেসে কনভার্ট করা হয়:
- প্রতিটি বাক্যের শব্দ ও বিষয়বস্তুর ওপর ভিত্তি করে একটি গাণিতিক ভেক্টর $\mathbf{v}_i$ তৈরি হয়।
- কাছাকাছি বিষয়ের বাক্যগুলোর ভেক্টর স্পেসে দিক (Direction) একই দিকে থাকে।

---

### ২. পাশাপাশি বাক্যের কোসাইন সিমিলারিটি পরিমাপ (Pairwise Similarity)
এরপর পাশাপাশি থাকা প্রতিটি বাক্যের ($S_i$ এবং $S_{i+1}$) মধ্যে **Cosine Similarity** মাপা হয়:

$$\text{Similarity}(S_i, S_{i+1}) = \frac{\mathbf{v}_i \cdot \mathbf{v}_{i+1}}{\|\mathbf{v}_i\| \|\mathbf{v}_{i+1}\|}$$

* **মান ১.০ এর কাছাকাছি:** দুটি বাক্য একই বিষয়ে কথা বলছে (যেমন: শেক্সপিয়রের জন্ম ও পরিবার)।
* **মান ০.০ এর কাছাকাছি:** বাক্যের বিষয়বস্তু হুট করে বদলে গেছে (টপিক শিফট)।

---

### ৩. সিমিলারিটি ড্রপ ডিটেকশন (Topic Shift / Cut Point)

```
সিমিলারিটি
  1.0 ─── S1 ─── S2 ─── S3                               S5 ─── S6
  0.8                      \                           /
  0.6                       \                         /
  0.4                        \                       /
  0.2                         S4 (ড্রপ! টপিক শিফট)
  0.0 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
      └────── চাঙ্ক ১ ──────┘   ✂️ (কাট পয়েন্ট)   └───── চাঙ্ক ২ ─────┘
      (শেক্সপিয়রের জন্ম ও বাড়ি)                (লন্ডনে থিয়েটার ও নাটক)
```

1. পুরো ডকুমেন্টের পাশাপাশি বাক্যগুলোর সিমিলারিটি স্কোরের ডিস্ট্রিবিউশন দেখে একটি **ড্রপ থ্রেশহোল্ড (১০ম পার্সেন্টাইল)** নির্ধারণ করা হয়।
2. **কাট পয়েন্ট নির্বাচন:** যতক্ষণ পর্যন্ত পাশাপাশি বাক্যের সিমিলারিটি বেশি থাকে, তারা একই চাঙ্কে যুক্ত হতে থাকে।
3. **কখন কাটা হয়:** যখনই সিমিলারিটিতে একটি বড় ধরনের ড্রপ বা পতন দেখা যায় (অর্থাৎ লেখক এক প্রসঙ্গ শেষ করে নতুন প্রসঙ্গে গেছেন), ঠিক সেখানেই আগের চাঙ্ক শেষ করে নতুন চাঙ্ক শুরু করা হয়।

### ১. বর্তমানে কোডে কীভাবে কাজ করছে?

[`chunker.py`](file:///e:/4-1%20Course%20Works/DL-429/Chunking_Package/chunker.py)-তে বায়োগ্রাফির বাক্যগুলোকে ভেক্টরাইজ করতে নিচের কোডটি রান হয়:

```python
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

# প্রতিটি বাক্যকে 1-gram ও 2-gram স্পার্স সেমান্টিক ভেক্টরে রূপান্তর
vec = TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), stop_words='english', sublinear_tf=True)
matrix = vec.fit_transform(sentences)

# পাশাপাশি দুটি বাক্যের ভেক্টরের ডট প্রোডাক্ট / কোসাইন সিমিলারিটি
sims = [(matrix[i] @ matrix[i+1].T).toarray()[0][0] for i in range(len(sentences) - 1)]
```

#### এটি কেন ব্যবহার করা হয়েছে?
1. **বিদ্যুৎ গতি ও জিরো ওভারহেড:** কোনো হেভি ডিপ লার্নিং মডেল বা GPU ছাড়া মাত্র কয়েক মিলি-সেকেন্ডে CPU-তে সম্পূর্ণ স্বাধীনভাবে চলে।

class ShakespeareRetriever:
    def __init__(self, index_path, chunk_ids_path, chunks_raw_path, model):
        self.index = faiss.read_index(index_path)
        self.chunk_ids = json.load(open(chunk_ids_path, encoding='utf-8'))
        raw_list = [json.loads(line) for line in open(chunks_raw_path, encoding='utf-8')]
        self.chunks_by_id = {c['id']: c for c in raw_list}
        self.model = model

    def search(self, query: str, top_k: int = 3):
        q_vec = self.model.encode([query], normalize_embeddings=True)
        scores, row_indices = self.index.search(q_vec.astype(np.float32), top_k)
        results = []
        for score, row_idx in zip(scores[0], row_indices[0]):
            cid = self.chunk_ids[row_idx]
            raw_c = self.chunks_by_id[cid]
            results.append((score, raw_c))
        return results

This transcript covers my work for Assignment 2, where I handled the Stage 3 OCR ground truth benchmarking and the Stage 4 hybrid chunking and vector index implementation.

I used the AI heavily during the initial exploration phase on Kaggle when traditional OCR engines completely fell apart on our scans. The plays have a tight two-column layout with heavy bleed-through from thin paper, and standard Tesseract runs gave us terrible error rates around 77% CER because it kept reading straight across both columns. I went back and forth with the AI testing smaller vision-language models on Kaggle GPU, including Florence-2 and GOT-OCR, before settling on Qwen. The AI was particularly helpful when debugging Kaggle runtime headaches, like when an updated Hugging Face tokenizer broke Florence-2 with attribute errors, or when PyTorch was taking over two minutes per page until we switched to SDPA and inference mode to get it down to around ~70 seconds. 

The most interesting thing I caught and fixed with prompt tuning was a recency bias bug with Qwen. When we put an abort instruction at the very end of the prompt telling it to output "[Blank Page]" if there was no text, the model got lazy on noisy pages and started labeling difficult scanned pages as blank. I had the AI restructure the prompt into an If/Otherwise gate so the abort check happened first, forcing the model to actually read the rest of the page. Once that was stable, our CER on verified test pages dropped below 4%.

For Stage 4, I didn't want naive fixed-token chunking because Shakespeare's text has very different structures across genres. I worked with the AI to build a hybrid pipeline: regex parsing for plays to group speeches by scene into roughly 200-word dialogue blocks with full citation headers, strict 1-to-1 chunking for the 140 sonnets, and embedding-similarity cuts using BGE-M3 for the long, unstructured paragraphs in the Biography section.

When it came time to integrate everything into our main submission codebase, I accepted the AI's help porting the logic into chunk.py, embed.py, and store.py, and setting up the FAISS index. I also caught a performance issue where our build_index.sh script was accidentally triggering the whole ingestion pipeline twice, which I had the AI clean up into a single pass. 

The main thing I pushed back on was repository management. The AI initially suggested saving all 1,360 extracted OCR text files directly into the git repo. That would have bloated the repository size unnecessarily, so I told it to write an automated download script in get_data.sh and add self-healing directory discovery in ocr.py instead. That way, the pipeline automatically pulls the pre-computed OCR dataset from Kaggle if it is missing locally, keeping our repo clean while ensuring graders can still run the index build seamlessly.
