"Layout" means figuring out where different kinds of content physically sit on a page,
before you try to read any of it. Is this region a paragraph of running text? A table? A
picture? A column boundary? You need that map before reading makes sense, the same way
you'd want to know a newspaper page has two columns before you start reading it left to
right across the whole width.

And that newspaper comparison isn't just a metaphor for you — it's literally your
situation. A large share of your corpus (the plays specifically) is printed in a genuine
two-column layout. A human reader instinctively knows to finish the entire left column
top to bottom, then move to the right column. A plain OCR engine, left to its own
devices, doesn't know that at all — by default it tends to scan roughly left to right
across the full width of the page, treating the page as if it were one wide single
column. That means it reads one line from the left column, then immediately reads
whatever happens to be physically next to it on the right column, then back to the left
column for the next line, over and over — producing text where two completely unrelated
pieces of dialogue get interleaved mid-sentence. That's not a minor annoyance, that's
often complete garbage that no downstream stage can recover meaning from.

The technical term for the gap between the two columns is the "gutter" — it's the
printer's word for that blank vertical strip. The fix is exactly what it sounds like:
detect where the gutter sits, split the page into a left region and a right region at
that boundary, read the left region fully top-to-bottom on its own, then read the right
region fully top-to-bottom on its own, then stitch the two results back together in the
order a human would actually read them.

For the tool side, there are two reasonable paths. One is to hand-write this splitting
logic yourself — since a two-column layout with a fairly regular gutter is a
comparatively simple, predictable pattern, a straightforward rule ("find the vertical
whitespace gap, split there") can work well without needing a trained model at all. The
other path is to use a general pretrained layout-detection model — something like
PP-Structure, which is built to automatically find text regions, tables, and columns on
a page without you writing the splitting rule yourself. The tradeoff is really about
robustness versus simplicity: a hand-written rule is easy to build and fast to run, but
it assumes your layout is fairly regular; a general model handles messier or more varied
layouts more gracefully, at the cost of being heavier to run and less transparent about
exactly what it's doing.

Stage 4 bundles two jobs together: chunking and embedding-plus-storage. Let's take them
one at a time, since they're conceptually pretty different from each other.

Chunking means cutting a page's text into smaller pieces before you try to search over
it. The reason you don't just search whole pages: if someone asks a narrow question,
retrieving an entire page as the "answer" forces whatever reads it next to sift through
a lot of irrelevant text to find the one relevant line. Smaller, well-chosen chunks let
your system retrieve just the relevant part. There are a couple of common ways to decide
where to cut: fixed-size chunking (every N words, regardless of what's actually being
said) is simple to implement but can slice a sentence — or worse, a whole dramatic
exchange — right down the middle. Semantic chunking, splitting at natural boundaries
like a new scene or a new speech, respects the actual structure of the text, at the cost
of being more work to implement since you need to actually detect those boundaries.

Embedding is the second half. An embedding model takes a piece of text and converts it
into a long list of numbers that captures its *meaning* rather than its literal wording —
so two passages about the same idea end up with similar numbers even if they don't share
a single word. Once every chunk has been turned into one of these number-lists, you need
somewhere to store all of them so that, given a new question (also converted into a
number-list), you can quickly find which stored chunks are closest in meaning. That
storage-and-search structure is called a vector index, and FAISS — a library originally
built at Meta — is one of the standard tools for this. FAISS actually offers more than
one indexing strategy: a "flat" index compares your query against literally every stored
vector, which is the most accurate method possible but scales linearly with corpus size,
versus an "approximate" index (things like HNSW or IVF) which trades a small amount of
accuracy for a lot of speed by cleverly skipping unlikely candidates — genuinely useful
once you're dealing with hundreds of thousands or millions of vectors, where flat search
would actually become too slow to be usable.

Your corpus has three distinct content types, and they demand different chunking

**1. Plays (Comedy, History, Tragedy) — Rule-based structural chunking.**

Plays are heavily structured: they have Act headings, Scene headings, and speaker

boundaries. A natural chunk is a sequence of speeches within the same scene, packed to a
target size (around 200 words) but never splitting a speech in half or crossing a scene
boundary. The algorithm works like this:
- First, you concatenate all pages of a play into a continuous text stream.
- You detect act and scene headers using regex patterns.
- You build a vocabulary of speaker names (by frequency, to filter out noise like

- You scan through the text, collecting speeches (speaker: text) until you reach the
  target word count or hit a scene change, then you flush that group as one chunk.
- The chunk carries metadata: title, act, scene, page range, speakers.

This is semantic chunking in the sense that it respects discourse units — each chunk is a
coherent block of dialogue within a single scene. It's rule-based because the structure
is already printed on the page, so you don't need a learned model to find the boundaries.

- Group sentences between cuts into chunks, again enforcing minimum and target word
  counts.

This is true "semantic chunking" in the modern RAG sense — boundaries are determined by
topic shifts, not by explicit formatting. The cost is that it requires embedding
inference during chunking (which is fine, since BGE-M3 is small and fast), and it's less
deterministic than rule-based, but it's the only way to get coherent chunks from
unstructured prose.

The reason we split the strategies is pragmatic: for plays, the structure is free and
reliable, so using it is fast, deterministic, and gives perfect citations. For biography,
there is no structure to lean on, so we use the more expensive but necessary semantic
method. That makes our chunking hybrid, which is actually a stronger design than a
one-size-fits-all approach.

That makes sense. I'm also curious about the embedding step — we chose BGE-M3, but you
mentioned earlier that it supports dense and sparse. How does that work in practice for
our corpus? And does it affect the index size or retrieval speed?

[AI]
Good questions. Let me give you the practical details.

BGE-M3 produces a 1024-dimensional dense vector for each chunk. That dense vector is what
we store in the FAISS index. The sparse representation is used only during reranking, not
during the initial index building. Here's how that plays out:

- During indexing, we only generate the dense embeddings and store them in the flat
  index. The index size for ~4,800 chunks × 1024 floats × 4 bytes/float = about 20 MB,
  plus a few MB for the chunk IDs and metadata. Tiny.

- During retrieval, the query is also embedded into a 1024-dim dense vector, and we do a
  brute-force inner product search against all stored vectors. This is fast — under 10ms
  on a CPU, and even faster on GPU.

- For reranking, we take the top, say, 20 candidates from the dense search. For each
  candidate, we compute a sparse vector (using BGE-M3's sparse encoder) and combine it
  with the dense score via a weighted sum (or max pooling). This adds a small overhead
  per query (since we only compute sparse vectors for the top candidates, not the whole
  corpus), but it often improves the rank of the true relevant chunk.

- The sparse component is essentially a learned bag-of-words: it captures exact term
  matches (like "honour" or "Falstaff") that might be diluted in the dense representation.
  For a literary corpus with many archaic terms, this can be quite helpful.

So the index itself is purely dense, and reranking uses the sparse as a post-hoc
refinement. This gives us the best of both worlds: fast retrieval via dense, and a
term-aware second pass to correct any ranking errors.

Now, about the agent loop — you mentioned earlier that we should have evidence-gated
re-search. Can you elaborate on that? How does the agent decide when to widen the search,
and when to stop and abstain?

[AI]
Yes, this is a critical part of making the agent robust. The idea is that the initial
retrieval might not always return a highly relevant chunk, especially for ambiguous or
broad questions. The agent should have a policy to widen its search when the evidence is
weak, but not endlessly.

Here's the concrete design we've committed to:

- The agent first calls retrieve with k = 5 (or some small number) and gets the top
  chunks and their similarity scores.
- It checks the top score against a threshold, say 0.35 (this is configurable). If the
  score is above the threshold, it proceeds with extraction and citation.
- If the score is below the threshold, the agent considers the evidence weak. It then
  calls retrieve again with k = 10, then k = 20 (each widening step increases k by a
  factor, or a fixed step). This allows the agent to cast a wider net.
- After three widening attempts (k = 5 → 10 → 20), if the top score is still below the
  threshold, the agent stops and calls escalate_to_human, or returns a polite "I couldn't
  find a relevant passage" response.

This is evidence-gated because the agent only widens when the evidence (score) is weak,
and it abstains when it has exhausted its search budget without finding anything
confident. This prevents the agent from hallucinating an answer when there is no support
in the corpus.

This design is directly tied to your NFR: robust/reliable. It ensures the agent doesn't
just guess; it only answers when it has high-confidence evidence, and it admits failure
gracefully.
