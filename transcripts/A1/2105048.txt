"Layout" means figuring out where different kinds of content physically sit on a page,
before you try to read any of it. Is this region a paragraph of running text? A table? A
picture? A column boundary? You need that map before reading makes sense, the same way
you'd want to know a newspaper page has two columns before you start reading it left to
right across the whole width.

And that newspaper comparison isn't just a metaphor for you — it's literally your
situation. A large share of your corpus (the plays specifically) is printed in a genuine
two-column layout. A human reader instinctively knows to finish the entire left column
top to bottom, then move to the right column. A plain OCR engine, left to its own
devices, doesn't know that at all — by default it tends to scan roughly left to right
across the full width of the page, treating the page as if it were one wide single
column. That means it reads one line from the left column, then immediately reads
whatever happens to be physically next to it on the right column, then back to the left
column for the next line, over and over — producing text where two completely unrelated
pieces of dialogue get interleaved mid-sentence. That's not a minor annoyance, that's
often complete garbage that no downstream stage can recover meaning from.

The technical term for the gap between the two columns is the "gutter" — it's the
printer's word for that blank vertical strip. The fix is exactly what it sounds like:
detect where the gutter sits, split the page into a left region and a right region at
that boundary, read the left region fully top-to-bottom on its own, then read the right
region fully top-to-bottom on its own, then stitch the two results back together in the
order a human would actually read them.

For the tool side, there are two reasonable paths. One is to hand-write this splitting
logic yourself — since a two-column layout with a fairly regular gutter is a
comparatively simple, predictable pattern, a straightforward rule ("find the vertical
whitespace gap, split there") can work well without needing a trained model at all. The
other path is to use a general pretrained layout-detection model — something like
PP-Structure, which is built to automatically find text regions, tables, and columns on
a page without you writing the splitting rule yourself. The tradeoff is really about
robustness versus simplicity: a hand-written rule is easy to build and fast to run, but
it assumes your layout is fairly regular; a general model handles messier or more varied
layouts more gracefully, at the cost of being heavier to run and less transparent about
exactly what it's doing.

Stage 4 bundles two jobs together: chunking and embedding-plus-storage. Let's take them
one at a time, since they're conceptually pretty different from each other.

Chunking means cutting a page's text into smaller pieces before you try to search over
it. The reason you don't just search whole pages: if someone asks a narrow question,
retrieving an entire page as the "answer" forces whatever reads it next to sift through a
lot of irrelevant text to find the one relevant line. Smaller, well-chosen chunks let your
system retrieve just the relevant part. There are a couple of common ways to decide where
to cut: fixed-size chunking (every N words, regardless of what's actually being said) is
simple to implement but can slice a sentence — or worse, a whole dramatic exchange — right
down the middle. Semantic chunking, splitting at natural boundaries like a new scene or a
new speech, respects the actual structure of the text, at the cost of being more work to
implement since you need to actually detect those boundaries.

Embedding is the second half. An embedding model takes a piece of text and converts it
into a long list of numbers that captures its *meaning* rather than its literal wording —
so two passages about the same idea end up with similar numbers even if they don't share
a single word. Once every chunk has been turned into one of these number-lists, you need
somewhere to store all of them so that, given a new question (also converted into a
number-list), you can quickly find which stored chunks are closest in meaning. That
storage-and-search structure is called a vector index, and FAISS — a library originally
built at Meta — is one of the standard tools for this. FAISS actually offers more than
one indexing strategy: a "flat" index compares your query against literally every stored
vector, which is the most accurate method possible but scales linearly with corpus size,
versus an "approximate" index (things like HNSW or IVF) which trades a small amount of
accuracy for a lot of speed by cleverly skipping unlikely candidates — genuinely useful
once you're dealing with hundreds of thousands or millions of vectors, where flat search
would actually become too slow to be usable.
